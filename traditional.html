What traditional coverage means here

The photograph your
grandmother asked for

There is a photograph at every South Indian wedding that has to exist. Everyone in the family knows which one it is.

Traditional coverage is the discipline of making sure those photographs are made properly — correctly lit, correctly composed, with the right people in frame and nobody blinking — while the ceremony goes on around them without being interrupted.

It means knowing the order of the rituals before the day starts, so the camera is already in the right place when the thaali is tied. It means understanding that the elders in the room are part of the record, not a background. And it means being willing to wait for a group to settle rather than hurrying them for the shot.

We shoot it alongside the candid work, not instead of it. You get the record and the story.

And it runs through every function, not only the muhurtham — the mehendi, the haldi, the sangeet, the beegara oota, the reception. Each one gets the same crew and the same attention.

  • Muhurtham and the core rituals — planned, positioned and lit before they begin.
  • Family and group portraits — composed properly, with the right people in the right order.
  • Mehendi, haldi and reception — each function covered with the same attention as the wedding.
  • Elders in frame — the relatives who travelled for this, photographed as part of the record.
  • Full ceremony coverage — start to finish, no gaps where the crew was elsewhere.
  • Hand-sequenced albums — designed so the story reads the way the day actually felt.
  • Four-week delivery — colour-graded, from the last event day.

The functions around the wedding

The mehendi, the haldi,
the beegara oota

Traditional coverage is not one day. It is every ritual that leads up to the muhurtham and every one that follows it — and each of them gets the same crew, the same planning and the same attention as the wedding itself.

The mehendi is not a warm-up. Neither is the haldi, the sangeet, the beegara oota or the reception.

Families often discover afterwards that the frames they return to most are not from the muhurtham at all — they are from the afternoon nobody thought to plan for, when the house was full and the cameras were expected to be somewhere else.

So we cover the whole sequence properly: every function on the calendar, photographed traditionally and candidly, and cut as its own short film if you want one.

  • Mehendi and haldi — the colour, the mess and the relatives who start it.
  • Sangeet — rehearsed and unrehearsed, both.
  • Beegara oota — the two families at one table, properly photographed.
  • Engagement and reception — stage portraits and the queue that forms around them.
  • Naming ceremonies, threading ceremonies and house warmings — the same discipline, a smaller room.
  • Full-length videography — the rituals uncut, for the family who want every minute.
